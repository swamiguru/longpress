oneLine: >-
  There is ==no such thing as a "digital arrest"==. The CBI, ED, police, customs and courts don't arrest anyone over a video call, and none of them will ask you to move money to prove you're innocent. If a call does either, hang up and report it.[^2]

applies:
  - who: A caller says your parcel, SIM or Aadhaar is linked to a crime
    status: act
    text: Hang up. That's how the scam opens. If you're worried, call the agency back on a number you find on its official website yourself.
  - who: You're on a video call and an "officer" says you can't hang up
    status: act
    text: You can. No agency keeps anyone in custody over Skype or WhatsApp. End the call and tell someone at home.[^2]
  - who: Money has already left your account
    status: act
    text: Call 1930 now, then file the details at cybercrime.gov.in. The sooner you report, the better the chance the money can be stopped before it moves on.[^1]
  - who: You got a suspicious call or message but lost nothing
    status: check
    text: Report the number on Chakshu, in the government's Sanchar Saathi portal, so it can be traced and cut off.[^3]
rules:
  - quote: "I4C advisory, October 2024: the CBI, ED, police, customs and judges do not arrest people through video calls."
    plain: If someone in uniform is arresting you over a video call, they aren't an officer.
  - quote: "PIB, 25 March 2025: 1930 is the toll-free helpline for lodging online cyber complaints, and cybercrime.gov.in covers all types of cyber crime."
    plain: After a loss there is one number to remember, 1930. Save it in your parents' phones today.
steps:
  - Hang up the moment a caller says you're under investigation and asks you to stay on camera.
  - Call the agency yourself, on a number from its official website. Never use a number the caller gives you.
  - Don't transfer money to "verify" your funds, "secure" your account or pay "bail". No agency asks for that.
  - If money has gone, call 1930 straight away, file at cybercrime.gov.in and call your bank's fraud line.
  - Talk to older relatives about this. The call works on fear and on keeping the victim alone, and the people with the most savings often lose the most.
finePrint:
  - The call usually starts with something ordinary, like a courier "held at customs", a SIM "used in a crime" or an Aadhaar number "linked to money laundering".
  - Scammers send fake arrest warrants and court letters on WhatsApp, complete with seals and signatures.
  - The "officer" will insist you stay on camera and tell nobody, including your family. That's to stop anyone from talking you out of it.
  - Money moves through mule accounts within minutes. That's why reporting fast matters more than anything else on this page.
unknowns:
  - There's no clean national count. The NCRB doesn't track digital arrest separately, so the figures in the news come from different sources and don't line up.[^1][^4]
  - Whether SIM-binding on messaging apps, due by 31 December 2026, will cut these calls down. It's meant to stop scammers reusing numbers, but many of the calls come from abroad.[^4]
take: >-
  By February 2025 the government had blocked 83,668 WhatsApp accounts and 3,962 Skype IDs linked to digital arrest, and the calls kept coming.[^1] That's because nearly half the operations run from Cambodia, Myanmar and Laos, out of reach of Indian police.[^4] Blocking accounts helps, but the cheapest protection is still a ten-minute conversation. Tell your parents that no real officer arrests anyone on a video call, and make sure 1930 is saved in their phones.
