    heading: "Your bank's zero-liability promise might not cover a digital arrest scam"
    body: >-
      RBI's zero-liability rule, the one most people quote after getting scammed, only covers transactions you didn't authorise, card skimming, an OTP used without your involvement. The second you type your own UPI PIN, even because a fake police officer told you to on a video call, the bank logs it as an authorised transaction. No zero-liability clock even starts. That's exactly the gap the [Supreme Court](https://www.freepressjournal.in/tech/indias-supreme-court-asks-whatsapp-to-list-measures-to-stop-digital-arrest-scams) wants banks and WhatsApp to close, with its next hearing on November 23.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-11-20/illustration_4.png"
    problem: >-
      Most people assume RBI's zero-liability rule means a bank has to refund any UPI scam, which shapes how calmly, or not, they react in the middle of a digital arrest call.
    breakthrough: >-
      RBI's Master Direction on customer protection limits liability only for unauthorised transactions. Digital arrest scams work specifically by getting the victim to authorise the transfer themselves, which is why banks usually classify it outside zero-liability protection, a gap the Supreme Court's ongoing suo motu case is trying to address, with WhatsApp and banks due back in court on November 23.
    catch: >-
      Some banks do make goodwill exceptions or settle disputes individually, and the legal picture could shift depending on how the Supreme Court case concludes. This isn't a guarantee you'll get nothing back, just that you shouldn't assume you automatically will.
    forYou: >-

    question: "Does RBI's zero-liability rule cover UPI digital arrest scams?"
    answer: >-
      No, usually not. RBI's zero-liability protection applies only to unauthorised transactions, ones you didn't approve, like card skimming. The moment you enter your own UPI PIN, even under a scammer's instructions during a fake video call, banks classify it as an authorised transaction, and the zero-liability clock never starts.
    qa:
      - q: "What does RBI's zero-liability rule actually cover?"
        a: "RBI's Master Direction on customer protection limits a customer's liability for unauthorised electronic transactions, ones where fraud, negligence or deficiency happened without the customer's own action, such as card cloning or an OTP intercepted without their involvement. It does not extend to transactions a customer themselves approved, authorised, or personally keyed in."
      - q: "Why doesn't zero-liability help digital arrest scam victims?"
        a: "Digital arrest scams work by getting the victim to personally authorise the transfer, entering their own UPI PIN or approving the payment while being threatened on a video call. Because the customer technically performed the action, banks typically classify it as authorised, not unauthorised, so the zero-liability protection doesn't automatically apply."
      - q: "What is the Supreme Court doing about this gap?"
        a: "The Supreme Court is hearing a suo motu case pushing banks, WhatsApp and other platforms to submit concrete prevention measures against digital arrest scams, with a hearing scheduled for November 23. The case is specifically trying to close the gap between official bank-liability rules and what actually happens to real scam victims."
