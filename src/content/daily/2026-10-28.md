    body: >-
      The Karnataka High Court has told the state's Cyber Command DGP to get Jio, Vodafone and other operators to audit how they appoint and supervise SIM distributors, in an order dated September 29 and reported on October 27. The case behind it is a Bengaluru promoter accused of opening extra SIMs on customers' Aadhaar details. Everyone talks about the buyer's side of SIM fraud. This order finally looks at the counter where the SIMs are sold, though it only asks for reviews, not penalties.
    problem: >-
      SIM fraud usually gets discussed as something that happens to you. A lot of it starts at the shop counter, with a seller who has your Aadhaar copy and a box of inactive SIMs.
    breakthrough: >-
      In a September 29 order [reported on October 27](https://www.medianama.com/2026/10/223-karnataka-hc-unauthorised-sim-activation/), Justice Suraj Govindaraj refused to quash a 2022 Bengaluru case against a former Jio promoter accused of using customers' Aadhaar numbers and photographs to activate extra SIMs. Police reportedly found 75, of which 55 were Jio and 20 Vodafone. The court then told the Cyber Command DGP to get operators to review distributor appointments, SIM-to-sale matching, repeat activations, ID checks and how customers report SIMs opened in their name. Compliance is due November 27.
    catch: >-
      It's a direction to review, not a rule with fines, and the man at the centre hasn't been found guilty of anything. The court also said the order doesn't allow indiscriminate surveillance or open access to customer data.
    forYou: >-
      Check which numbers are registered against your Aadhaar on sancharsaathi.gov.in, and report any you don't recognise to your operator. It takes two minutes and nobody else will do it for you.
    question: "What did the Karnataka High Court order telecom operators to review about SIM activations?"
    answer: >-
      In a September 29 order reported on October 27, 2026, the Karnataka High Court asked the state's Cyber Command DGP to direct Jio, Vodafone and other operators to review how SIM distributors are appointed, supervised and matched to sales, and how unauthorised activations are caught and reported. A compliance report is due November 27.

    body: >-
      As of October 2026, Airtel checks the links you open on its mobile network and Wi-Fi and blocks scam domains with a warning page, with no app and no setting to switch on.     problem: >-
      Most scam links arrive on WhatsApp or SMS, where you only find out it was fake after the tap.
    breakthrough: >-
      Airtel's [network fraud filter](https://airtelcms.airtel.in/spam-fighting-network/) checks the domain of any link you click on its mobile network or Wi-Fi in real time. Malicious ones are blocked with a warning page, whether the link came from SMS, chat, social media or search. Suspected spam calls and SMS get an 'Airtel Warning: SPAM' label on VoLTE phones, built from call patterns and suspicious URLs.
    catch: >-
      It only protects you on Airtel's own network, can't be turned on or off by you, and a VPN or custom DNS can bypass the link check. It's a safety net and doesn't replace not tapping.
    forYou: >-
      On Airtel: leave VPN and custom DNS off unless you need them. Link wrongly blocked, mail spam.grievance@airtel.com. Already scammed, report at sancharsaathi.gov.in and call your bank.
    question: "How does Airtel block scam links and can a VPN switch that protection off?"
    answer: >-
      Airtel checks links you open on its mobile network or Wi-Fi and blocks malicious domains with a warning page, with no app or setting involved. A VPN or custom DNS can stop that check from running, so on Airtel, leave both off unless you actually need them.

    heading: "Digital arrest isn't a thing, it's a script"
    body: >-
      A 69-year-old retired railway officer in Lucknow was held on a nine-day 'digital arrest' and lost Rs 47 lakh between October 15 and 23, according to a complaint reported on October 27. There's no such thing as a digital arrest. It's a script, and the part that gives it away is the demand for secrecy. Police don't ask you to stay on camera and tell nobody, and the only thing that moves when you follow the script is your money.
    problem: >-
      The scam works because the people it targets are told to stop thinking and keep quiet, and the fear is built to feel official.
    breakthrough: >-
      In a case [reported on October 27](https://the420.in/retired-railway-officer-digital-arrest-fraud/), Alok Kumar Sharma, 69, got a WhatsApp call on October 15 from a woman claiming to be a Lucknow Police inspector, who said a SIM in his name had been used to fund a terrorist. A fake ATS officer from Pune then took over on video, he was told to keep his camera on and tell no one, and he broke his fixed deposits to move the money to designated accounts. He lost Rs 47 lakh before he spoke to an acquaintance who called it extortion. The cybercrime police station registered an FIR against unidentified suspects.
    catch: >-
      Police are still tracing the accounts and numbers, and nobody has been named. Money moved this way is hard to recover once it has gone through several accounts.
    forYou: >-
      If anyone on a call says you're under arrest, hang up. Tell family, call 1930 or report on the national cybercrime portal, and never move money to prove you're innocent.
    question: "Is digital arrest real in India and what do I do?"
    answer: >-
      No. Real police don't arrest anyone over WhatsApp or video calls, and no agency asks you to stay on camera and tell nobody. A 69-year-old retired railway officer in Lucknow reportedly lost Rs 47 lakh between October 15 and 23 this way. Hang up and call 1930.

      - q: "What should I do if I get a digital arrest call in India?"
        a: "Hang up, pay nothing and tell someone in your family straight away. Report the number on the national cybercrime portal or call 1930. If money has already moved, tell your bank at once, because early reports give the best chance of freezing the transfers."
