      Every big sale season, the same trick shows up: a product's 'original price' quietly climbs right before the discount badge appears, so a modest markdown reads like a huge one. Amazon's in-app Price History feature is built to catch that. Open any product page, tap Price History, and you get up to 365 days of what the item has actually sold for, not what today's banner claims. Don't want to dig through the page? Just ask Rufus, Amazon's built-in AI assistant, 'has this price dropped before,' and it tells you directly. It isn't bulletproof. Some sellers still rotate listings or tweak variants to dodge a clean price trail, so a chart with no real history isn't proof either way. But for most products, it turns 'biggest sale of the year' from a slogan into a number you can check before you add to cart.

    problem: >-

    breakthrough: >-
      Amazon's app has quietly built in a Price History view that shows up to 365 days of a product's actual price, reachable from the product page or by asking the Rufus AI assistant directly whether a price has dropped before.
    catch: >-

    body: >-
      There is no law in India that says when the police can crack open your phone. Not a statute, not a published rule, nothing that specifies what can be copied off the device, how long the data sits in a file somewhere, or who gets to read it. The Internet Freedom Foundation dug this up through an RTI request: Home Minister Amit Shah personally signed off on a one-time exemption from Make in India procurement rules so the National Investigation Agency could buy a Cellebrite Premium Tool, a three-year licence running about Rs 4.98 crore, awarded to a company called Nextechno Gen. The tool's own specification calls for brute-forcing passcodes across most Android and iPhone models and pulling a device's entire file system, including protected areas like Samsung's Secure Folder. Cellebrite's tools have shown up in Serbia, Jordan, Kenya and Russia, used against journalists and activists, according to researchers who've tracked them. Fifteen separate Cellebrite-related tenders appear across Indian agencies this year alone, from the Serious Fraud Investigation Office to state forensic departments. And then there's Dhruv Rathee, detained by Delhi Police on October 10 ahead of a protest, his phone and his parents' phones taken. He says officers may have tried to image his iPhone. Unverified, by IFF's own account. What isn't unverified is that the tool exists, the money's been spent, and nobody can point you to the rule that's supposed to govern any of it.
