    heading: "Chrome Can Track Prices For You"
    body: >-
      Chrome can track a product's price for you without any extension, a feature [Google](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/11625545) has documented on its own support pages for years but that almost nobody actually turns on. Open a product page on a supported shopping site, bookmark it like normal, and a "Track price" toggle shows up in that bookmark panel. Flip it on, and Chrome checks the price in the background, notifying you the moment it drops, right from your Reading List or bookmarks, on both desktop and Android. No sign-up with a third-party tracker, no app reading your shopping habits, just Chrome quietly doing the one thing shopping apps charge you attention for.

    heading: "Myth: Sale Price Is Cheapest"
    body: >-
      [India's Central Consumer Protection Authority](https://consumeraffairs.nic.in/sites/default/files/file-uploads/latestnews/Draft%20Guidelines%20for%20Prevention%20and%20Regulation%20of%20Dark%20Patterns%202023.pdf) has flagged a dark pattern worth knowing before the year-end sales: retailers quietly raising a product's "original" price for a few weeks, then running a festive sale against that inflated number so the discount looks bigger than it is. The result is a sale price that can be no cheaper, sometimes even pricier, than what the same item sold for three months earlier. The fix costs ten seconds: check the price history before you buy, through the retailer's own tool where one exists or a tracking app, and compare today's number to a few months back. If today's "deal" isn't actually lower, it's not a discount, it's a sticker.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-11-21/illustration_5.png"
    problem: >-
      Shoppers trust the big red discount percentage during festive sales, but that number is only as honest as the "original" price it's measured against.
    breakthrough: >-
      India's dark-patterns guidelines from the Department of Consumer Affairs explicitly call out inflated reference pricing, where a retailer raises the listed price shortly before a sale so the markdown looks larger than the real saving.
    catch: >-
      There's no single tool that catches every case, and enforcement on price inflation specifically is thinner than on other dark patterns like fake urgency timers, so the burden mostly falls on the shopper to check.
    forYou: >-
      Before you buy anything in the next round of sales, pull up its price history for the last two to three months. If today's number isn't actually lower, skip the "deal" and wait.
    question: "Is the Diwali or Black Friday sale price always the lowest of the year?"
    answer: >-
      No. India's Consumer Protection dark-patterns guidelines flag the practice of inflating a product's "original" price in the weeks before a sale, which makes the advertised discount look bigger than the actual saving, so a festive-sale price is not automatically the cheapest you'll see all year.
    qa:
      - q: "How can I check if a sale price is actually a good deal?"
        a: "Pull up the product's price history, through a retailer's own tool where available or a price-tracking app, and compare today's price to what it was two or three months ago. If it isn't lower, the \"discount\" is just marketing."
      - q: "Is inflating prices before a sale illegal in India?"
        a: "India's Central Consumer Protection Authority has issued guidelines against dark patterns, including false discounts and fake urgency, and has already fined companies like Ola for similar practices, though enforcement on price inflation specifically is still developing."
