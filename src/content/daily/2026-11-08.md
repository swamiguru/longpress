    heading: "UPI Above ₹2,000 Isn't Free"
    body: >-
      NPCI told the Supreme Court on November 5 that merchants cannot pass UPI's new MDR fee onto customers, and the court didn't buy the merchants' request to pause the charge either. Here's what actually changed back in October: big merchants now pay NPCI 0.4% on UPI payments above ₹2,000, a fee meant to keep the free-UPI promise intact by taxing the payment chain instead of your wallet. Except that's not quite how pricing works. A fee that hits a shop's margin has a way of finding its way back to the price tag, MDR rule or not. The court case won't settle that argument, just whether NPCI can legally insist it tries.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-11-08/illustration_1.png"
    problem: >-
      UPI has always been free for the person paying, but somebody has to fund the rails underneath it. NPCI's answer was a merchant-side fee, and now that fee is in court.
    breakthrough: >-
      On November 5, NPCI told the Supreme Court merchants cannot legally add the 0.4% MDR charge to a customer's bill on UPI payments over ₹2,000, introduced from October 15, 2026. The [court](https://www.livelaw.in/top-stories/merchants-cannot-pass-on-upi-charges-to-customers-mdr-necessary-to-sustain-upi-ecosystem-npci-tells-supreme-court-557510) declined to stay the fee while it hears the case and asked the Centre to explain the rule.
    catch: >-
      A rule on paper and a rule that survives contact with a shopkeeper's calculator are two different things, and nobody's watching every bill.
    forYou: >-
      If a shop tries to add a UPI surcharge on a payment over ₹2,000, that's against NPCI's own rule, not a new tax you owe. Push back, or pay another way instead.
    question: "Do I have to pay to use UPI above ₹2,000?"
    answer: >-
      Only at certain large merchants, and only above ₹2,000, since mid-October 2026. NPCI charges those merchants a 0.4% MDR fee. On November 5, NPCI told the Supreme Court that merchants legally can't push that cost onto you, though the court declined to block the fee while it hears the case.
    qa:
      - q: "What is UPI MDR and why was it introduced?"
        a: "MDR, the Merchant Discount Rate, is the 0.4% fee NPCI charges large merchants on bigger UPI payments. It kicked in from October 15, 2026 to help fund the payment infrastructure, since UPI itself earns nothing from small, person-to-person transfers, which stay free."
      - q: "Can shops legally charge me extra for paying by UPI above ₹2,000?"
        a: "No. NPCI explicitly told the Supreme Court on November 5 that merchants cannot pass the MDR fee to customers. If you spot a surcharge for UPI on a bill over ₹2,000, that merchant is breaking NPCI's own rule, not following a new one."
      - q: "Does the MDR fee apply to UPI transfers between friends and family?"
        a: "No. The 0.4% fee only applies to person-to-merchant transactions above ₹2,000 at specific large merchant categories. Peer-to-peer transfers, small shops below the threshold, and most everyday UPI use stay completely free, exactly as before."

    heading: "India Wrote AI Rules. Now What"
    body: >-
      CERT-In published a 107-page AI Security Auditing framework on November 5, and most of it is aimed squarely at agentic AI, the kind that plans tasks, uses tools, and sometimes hands work off to other AI systems without asking first. The checklist is genuinely thorough: test whether an agent exceeds its permissions, whether its memory can leak one user's data to another, whether a shutdown command actually shuts it down. What the guidelines don't have yet is teeth. No named compliance deadline, no stated penalty, no list of who's actually required to run one of these audits. India built the rulebook before it decided who has to follow it, and that order of operations is going to matter the first time an AI agent does something nobody approved.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-11-08/illustration_4.png"
    problem: >-
      AI agents are already booking, paying and messaging on people's behalf, and until now nobody in India had written down how you're even supposed to test whether one is safe.
    breakthrough: >-
      [CERT-In](https://letsdatascience.com/news/cert-in-issues-ai-security-auditing-guidelines-covering-agen-6e37b2b9) released a 107-page AI Security Auditing Guidelines framework on November 5, 2026, built specifically around agentic AI: checking permissions, task delegation, shared memory leaks between users, shutdown controls, and full audit logs of what an agent actually did.
    catch: >-
      It reads like a serious checklist for auditors, but it names no compliance deadline, no penalty, and no clear list of who must actually get audited.
    forYou: >-
      Nothing changes for you today. But the next time an AI assistant books something you didn't ask for, this is the document that decides whether anyone's actually checking for that.
    question: "What do CERT-In's new AI Security Auditing Guidelines actually require?"
    answer: >-
      Published November 5, 2026, CERT-In's guidelines tell auditors how to test AI systems, especially agentic ones that act on their own, for risks like exceeding permissions, unsafe task delegation, memory leaks between users, and broken emergency-stop mechanisms. They don't yet specify a binding compliance deadline or penalties.
    qa:
      - q: "Does this mean every AI app in India must now pass a government audit?"
        a: "Not yet. CERT-In's document is a guideline for how audits should be done, covering things like permission checks, shutdown controls and audit logs for agentic AI, not a law mandating who must be audited or by when. Enforcement and binding scope are still unclear."
      - q: "What's an agentic AI system that this is really targeting?"
        a: "It's AI that plans and carries out multi-step tasks on its own, uses outside tools, or hands off work to other AI agents, think an assistant that can book, pay or message without a human approving each step. Those are exactly the systems the guidelines focus on testing."
      - q: "Why does a government guideline matter if nothing's mandatory yet?"
        a: "Because guidelines like this usually become the template regulators and banks lean on once something goes wrong, the same way RBI's cybersecurity audits built on earlier CERT-In frameworks. It's the first sign India is thinking about AI accountability before, not after, an agent misfires."
