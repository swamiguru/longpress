    heading: "Algorithms may lose their shield"
    body: >-
      The Indian government is weighing whether to strip Section 79 safe harbour protection from platforms whose algorithms decide what content gets ranked, boosted or suppressed, according to a November 5 report by [MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/11/223-government-safe-harbour-social-media-algorithm/). A senior government official told Business Standard that an intermediary with an active role in deciding its own fate needs its blanket protection relooked at. The same discussion includes a separate plan letting users ask platforms directly to remove deepfakes or non-consensual content, without first needing a police complaint or court order. Both ideas are still at a preliminary stage and would require amending the IT Act.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-11-07/illustration_2.png"
    problem: >-
      Section 79 currently gives platforms broad protection from liability for what users post, as long as they don't initiate transmissions or modify content and meet due diligence rules, a shield that applies whether or not the platform's algorithm is actively deciding what millions of people see.
    breakthrough: >-
      Officials are now arguing that algorithmic ranking is itself an active editorial choice, not neutral hosting, so platforms that rank, promote or suppress content could lose blanket protection while plain peer-to-peer messaging apps that don't interfere with messages might stay exempt. A parallel proposal would let users demand deepfake takedowns directly.
    catch: >-
      This is early-stage discussion, not a bill. Digital rights advocates and creators have already flagged a risk: platforms facing more liability may respond by over-removing or shadow-banning contentious content pre-emptively, rather than making smarter ranking decisions.
    forYou: >-
      If you run a page or account that depends on algorithmic reach, this is worth tracking before it becomes law, since it could change how aggressively platforms moderate or throttle content in India.
    question: "Will India remove safe harbour protection for social media algorithms?"
    answer: >-
      Not yet. As of November 2026, the Indian government is only considering withdrawing blanket Section 79 safe harbour from platforms whose algorithms rank, boost or suppress content. Any change needs an IT Act amendment and stakeholder consultation first, so nothing is confirmed or dated.
    qa:
      - q: "Which platforms would lose safe harbour protection in India?"
        a: "Social media intermediaries that use algorithms to decide ranking, placement, promotion or suppression of user content, think Instagram, YouTube and X, would be the target. Peer-to-peer messaging services that don't interfere with messages between users, like basic WhatsApp chats, could stay exempt under the proposal as currently described."
      - q: "What is Section 79 of India's IT Act?"
        a: "Section 79 gives intermediaries conditional protection from liability for content users post, as long as the platform doesn't initiate transmissions, select recipients, or modify information, and meets due diligence rules. It doesn't cover platforms that abet unlawful acts or ignore a government or court takedown order."
      - q: "Can Indian users already get deepfakes removed without a police complaint?"
        a: "Generally no, removing a deepfake currently usually needs a law enforcement complaint or court order. The government is separately considering a direct takedown mechanism so users can ask platforms to remove deepfakes or non-consensual images themselves, though officials say speed matters and the plan isn't finalised."

    body: >-
      A Delhi consumer commission has ruled that entering an OTP, MPIN or UPI PIN during a fraud doesn't automatically make the loss the customer's fault, ordering Canara Bank to refund Rs 4.14 lakh plus compensation to a woman who lost Rs 5.44 lakh to a fraudulent call in April 2024, according to [The420](https://the420.in/canara-bank-cyber-fraud-refund-consumer-commission-order/). The commission, in an order dated October 28, 2026, found that successful technical authentication did not necessarily establish voluntary authorisation, rejecting the bank's argument that correctly-entered credentials settled the matter.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-11-07/illustration_5.png"
    problem: >-
      Banks routinely deny cyber fraud refund claims by pointing to the fact that the OTP, PIN or MPIN was entered correctly, treating that as proof the customer authorised the transaction and was therefore negligent.
    breakthrough: >-
      The commission relied on an NCDRC precedent distinguishing technical authentication from genuine consent, and found Canara Bank hadn't produced IP logs, device verification records, beneficiary-addition logs or audit trails to actually prove the customer authorised the transfer herself. The order adds Rs 1 lakh for mental agony and Rs 50,000 in costs, with 45 days to pay.
    catch: >-
      This doesn't mean banks always lose such cases, screen-sharing with a fraudster can still count as negligence, and the commission acknowledged that. It means the bank has to actually prove it, not just point at a matched PIN.
    forYou: >-
      If a bank ever tells you a case is closed because the OTP matched, this ruling is worth citing: ask what evidence they have beyond that single fact.
    question: "Can a bank refuse a fraud refund just because the OTP was entered?"
    answer: >-
      Not automatically. A Delhi consumer commission ruled in October 2026 that entering an OTP, MPIN or UPI PIN proves technical authentication, not voluntary consent. It ordered Canara Bank to refund Rs 4.14 lakh plus compensation because the bank couldn't prove the customer genuinely authorised the transfer.
    qa:
      - q: "What evidence does a bank need to deny a fraud claim?"
        a: "Courts have asked for concrete technical evidence: IP logs, device verification records, beneficiary-addition logs and transaction audit trails showing the customer's own device and behaviour, not just that the correct OTP or PIN was entered somewhere."
      - q: "How long does a bank have to pay after such an order?"
        a: "In this case, the Delhi commission gave Canara Bank 45 days to comply, with 9 percent annual interest on the refund from the date of the fraud, rising to 12 percent if the bank misses that deadline."
