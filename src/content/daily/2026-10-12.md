    heading: "TCS keeps hiring. Its dollar revenue barely moved."
    body: >-
      TCS's September-quarter numbers are a decent reality check on the 'AI will wipe out Indian IT' talk. Headcount rose by 4,258 to about 5.98 lakh, the third straight quarter of net additions, and the company puts its AI business at a $3.1 billion annual run rate. But dollar revenue grew just 2.4% from a year ago. If I worked there, that number would worry me more than any chatbot.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-10-12/illustration_2.png"
    problem: >-
      For two years the fear around Indian IT has been that AI tools would let clients do more with fewer engineers, and TCS's workforce shrank in FY26, which fed that story.
    breakthrough: >-
      In results out on October 8, TCS reported Q2 FY27 revenue of Rs 73,188 crore, up 11.2% in rupees, and net profit of Rs 13,934 crore, according to [Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/markets/stocks/story/tcs-adds-4258-employees-in-q2-marks-third-quarter-of-headcount-addition-560460-2026-10-08). The company's own [fact sheet](https://www.tcs.com/content/dam/tcs/investor-relations/financial-statements/2026-27/q2/Presentations/Q2%202026-27%20Fact%20Sheet.pdf) shows dollar revenue of $7.64 billion, up 2.4% year on year and 2.8% in constant currency, with a $9.6 billion order book and attrition down to 13.3%.
    catch: >-
      Rupee growth flatters the picture because a weaker rupee inflates it. The AI run-rate figure is TCS's own and doesn't say how much of it is new money versus old projects relabelled.
    forYou: >-
      If you're job-hunting in IT, hiring is steady rather than collapsing, but slow growth means raises and fresher pay stay tight. HCLTech reports today and Infosys on October 23, which will show whether this is TCS or the whole sector.

    heading: "Don't just block the scam number. Report it on Chakshu."
    body: >-
      Blocking a scam number protects you. Reporting it can protect the next person. In the Sanchar Saathi app or on sancharsaathi.gov.in, open Chakshu, pick whether it came as a call, SMS or WhatsApp, add the number, the time and a screenshot, and verify with an OTP. The Department of Telecommunications checks it and can disconnect the sender.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-10-12/illustration_3.png"
    problem: >-
      Most of us block a fake 'bank' caller and move on, which leaves the same number free to try the next hundred people.
    breakthrough: >-
      Chakshu is DoT's reporting tool inside Sanchar Saathi for suspected fraud calls, SMS and WhatsApp messages, covering financial fraud, impersonation and fake offers. As [Factly](https://factly.in/explainer-government-launches-chakshu-to-report-sms-call-or-whatsapp-messages-attempting-to-commit-fraud/amp) explains, DoT verifies each report and can re-verify the subscriber, cut the connection or refer it to police, and the reporter's identity stays confidential.
    catch: >-
      It's for attempts, not losses. If money has already left your account, Chakshu won't help you get it back, so call 1930 or file at cybercrime.gov.in straight away.
    forYou: >-
      Next time a dodgy call or APK lands on WhatsApp, take the screenshot before you block, then spend two minutes on Chakshu.

    heading: "Your bank will never send you an app on WhatsApp"
    body: >-
      Haryana's Rewari Police put out a warning on October 11 about .apk files sent over WhatsApp, Telegram and SMS by people posing as bank, courier or electricity staff. Install one and the malware sits in the background reading OTPs and opening your banking apps. The fix is boring and it works: keep 'Install unknown apps' off, install only from the Play Store, and never put AnyDesk on your phone because 'customer care' asked.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-10-12/illustration_4.png"
    problem: >-
      APK malware gets past the usual advice about not sharing OTPs, because once it's installed, nobody has to ask you for anything. It just reads them.
    breakthrough: >-
      Rewari SP Hemendra Kumar Meena issued the advisory during Cyber Security Month, saying these cases are rising quickly, per [The Tribune](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/haryana/beware-of-apk-files-received-through-whatsapp-sms-police-warn-residents/). The files arrive dressed up as a bank KYC update, a courier tracking app or an electricity bill, and give criminals remote access to messages, contacts, the gallery and banking apps.
    catch: >-
      Android warns you before sideloading, but people tap through warnings when the message looks urgent and the sender sounds official. Older parents are the usual target, which makes this a family conversation more than a settings change.
    forYou: >-
      Check that 'Install unknown apps' is off for WhatsApp and your browser on every Android phone at home. If someone has already lost money, call 1930 and file at cybercrime.gov.in.

    heading: "Nadella wants an AI emergency brake. It should've shipped first."
    body: >-
      Satya Nadella posted on Saturday that we should 'assume a model is compromised and contain it from the start', with an authorised human able to pause it mid-task. He called it an emergency brake. He's right, and tamper-proof logs plus a kill switch outside the model are basic engineering. What bugs me is the order. Agents are already inside Office and Windows, and the brake is still a post on X.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-10-12/illustration_5.png"
    problem: >-
      AI agents now take actions, not just answer questions, and the usual setup trusts the model to police itself, which falls apart the moment it's tricked by a poisoned document or simply gets things wrong.
    breakthrough: >-
      In an X post on October 10, Nadella called for separating the model from the harness that runs it, moving safeguards outside the model, logging every meaningful action as 'tamper-proof human readable evidence', and letting an authorised person shut a model down mid-task, as [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/10/microsofts-satya-nadella-says-ai-models-need-an-emergency-brake/) reported.
    catch: >-
      A CEO post isn't a product spec. There's no date, no standard and no promise that Microsoft's own Copilot agents will meet this bar, and the industry has a habit of writing safety principles after the launch rather than before it.
    forYou: >-
      If your company is rolling out AI agents, ask the boring questions now: who can stop it, where are the logs, and can the agent edit them. If nobody knows, that's your answer.
