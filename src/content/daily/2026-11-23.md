    body: >-
      The government quietly dropped its demand for a call kill switch on digital arrest scams, and WhatsApp's weaker alternative won. Meeting minutes filed in the Supreme Court's own case show a September sit-down where WhatsApp and Telegram talked the kill switch down to [an option](https://theprint.in/tech/exclusive-centre-backs-down-on-kill-switch-for-digital-arrests-whatsapps-warnings-first-plan-prevails/3096987/), no timeline attached. WhatsApp's argument wasn't wrong, exactly: over 99% of calls running past an hour are legitimate, and anyone running a scam just hangs up at minute 59. But that's also the argument of every company that doesn't want a feature that costs it users. What's left is a warning pop-up and a toggle you have to turn on yourself. The case is back in court today. Watch what, if anything, changes.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-11-23/illustration_1.png"
    problem: >-
      Digital arrest scams keep India's courts and cybercrime desks busy: fraudsters impersonate police or customs officers on long video calls and extort money while victims are too scared to hang up. The Supreme Court has been running a dedicated case since October 2025 trying to force platforms into real preventive measures, not just awareness campaigns.
    breakthrough: >-
      Minutes from a September 9, 2026 inter-ministerial meeting, filed in the Union home ministry's fifth status report, show the government dropped its push for an automatic kill switch on calls running past a set duration. Only India's own cybercrime coordination centre, I4C, still wanted it; WhatsApp called it a blunt tool and said scammers would simply end calls just before any cutoff, while Telegram called it very hard to build. MeitY instead adopted a graded approach built on what the platforms already offered: warning pop-ups on long calls, and an opt-in toggle to silence unknown callers.
    catch: >-
      The kill switch isn't dead, just demoted to a future option with no call duration, timeline, or platform named, so this isn't really a plan at all. Telcos were already disconnecting calls at up to 180 minutes before any of this, so the win here is mostly paperwork.
    forYou: >-
      Turn on Silence Unknown Callers in WhatsApp's privacy settings yourself; it's the one part of this that already works, and the government isn't going to make the platform do it for you. The case returns to the Supreme Court today, November 23.
    question: "Did India drop the WhatsApp call kill switch for digital arrest scams?"
    answer: >-
      Yes. Government meeting records show the Centre dropped its demand for an automatic kill switch on long calls after WhatsApp and Telegram argued it wouldn't work. WhatsApp's own warnings-first plan, mostly pop-ups and an opt-in silence-unknown-callers toggle, won instead. The case returns to the Supreme Court on November 23, 2026.

      Google Photos has had a genuinely useful privacy feature sitting in its menus for years, and most people have never opened it. Library, then Utilities, then Locked Folder lets you move any photo or video out of your main library into a folder that needs your phone's own lock screen to open, and once it's in there it vanishes from search, memories, and anything you've shared. It's not encryption-grade security, but it's miles better than hoping nobody scrolls too far on your camera roll.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-11-23/illustration_3.png"
    problem: >-
      Everyone has a few photos, IDs, documents, or screenshots they don't want showing up when someone else swipes through their gallery, or when Google Photos surfaces an old memory at the worst time.
    breakthrough: >-
      Google Photos' [Locked Folder](https://support.google.com/photos/answer/10694388) moves selected photos and videos behind your phone's own screen lock, removing them from the main grid, search, memories, and shared albums entirely.
    catch: >-
      It's gated by your lock screen, not a separate password, so it's only as secure as your PIN or fingerprint setup, and backup behaviour to the cloud varies by device and Android version.
    forYou: >-
      If you've never opened Utilities in the Google Photos app, that's where this and a few other quietly useful tools live. Worth five minutes the next time you're waiting for a chai.
    question: "How do I hide private photos in Google Photos without deleting them?"
    answer: >-
      Open Google Photos, go to Library, then Utilities, then Locked Folder, set it up with your phone's screen lock, then move any photo or video into it. Locked items disappear from your main grid, search, memories and shared albums until you unlock the folder again.
    qa:
      - q: "Does Locked Folder back up to Google's servers?"
        a: "On newer Android versions you can choose to back it up; on many devices it stays local-only by default, which is the point if you don't want sensitive images anywhere near the cloud. Check your own phone's behaviour inside Locked Folder's settings before assuming either way."
      - q: "Can anyone see my Locked Folder photos if they pick up my phone?"
        a: "Not without your screen lock PIN, pattern, fingerprint or face unlock, since Locked Folder piggybacks on whatever you already use to unlock your phone. There's no separate password to forget, which is also why it's only as secure as your lock screen."

    heading: "The fastest way to limit digital arrest damage takes 30 seconds"
    body: >-
      Digital arrest scams work by keeping someone scared and on the phone long enough to move money before they can think straight, and most of that money moves over UPI. Every major UPI app lets you set your own daily or per-transaction limit below the bank's default of around one lakh rupees, in your profile or payment settings, and most people have never touched it. It's not a fix for the scam itself. It's a ceiling on how much damage one panicked transfer can do before someone around you, or you yourself an hour later, realizes what happened.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-11-23/illustration_4.png"
    problem: >-
      Digital arrest and similar scams rely on panic and speed, pushing victims to transfer large sums over UPI before they have time to question what's happening.
    breakthrough: >-
      Every major UPI app and most bank apps let users [set a personal daily or per-transaction limit](https://paytm.com/faqs/upi/how-to-set-a-limit-for-upi-transactions) below the system default, which currently sits around one lakh rupees for most banks, directly in their profile or payment settings.
    catch: >-
      This caps damage, it doesn't prevent the scam itself, and it only helps if the limit is actually lower than what a scammer could otherwise push through in one sitting.
    forYou: >-
      Set your limit to something comfortably above your real daily spending but well under the default, so normal life doesn't get disrupted but a worst-case transfer gets capped.
    question: "How do I lower my UPI daily transaction limit to protect against scams?"
    answer: >-
      Open your UPI app, such as PhonePe, Google Pay or Paytm, go to your profile or payment settings, find your linked bank account, and set a lower daily UPI limit than the default, which is often one lakh rupees. It won't stop a scam call, but it caps what a pressured transfer can move.
    qa:
      - q: "Where exactly do I find the UPI limit setting?"
        a: "It varies slightly by app: in Paytm it's under UPI settings in your profile, in Google Pay it's under your bank account details, and in PhonePe it's under your profile's payment methods. Look for set transaction limit or UPI limit near your linked bank account."
      - q: "Does lowering my UPI limit affect normal payments?"
        a: "Only if you set it below what you actually spend day to day, so pick a number comfortably above your normal usage but well under the full default, then raise it back temporarily the rare time you need to send more."
