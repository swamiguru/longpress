    body: >-
      Geely unveiled a 2.2 megawatt charging system that takes a compatible EV battery to roughly 70% in about four and a half minutes, which is around 50% more power than BYD's newest system. It is real engineering and a sign of how fast Chinese carmakers are moving. The catch is that brands choose the percentage range that sounds best, and none of it matters without megawatt chargers on the other end.
    problem: >-
      Charging time is still the thing that makes people hesitate on EVs. Twenty or thirty minutes at a fast charger feels like forever next to a five-minute fuel stop.
    breakthrough: >-
      Geely showed the system at its Ningbo safety centre on September 23: a charger rated at about 2.2MW that fills a battery to around 70% in roughly 4.5 minutes, with Geely quoting 10 to 97% in under nine minutes. BYD's latest flash charging tops out near 1.5MW. Geely also says its AI-managed charging extends battery cycle life, though that is a company claim for now.
    catch: >-
      Headline charging times depend heavily on where you start and stop, and a 2MW charger needs serious grid capacity that very few places have. In India, most public fast chargers deliver a small fraction of that power, so don't expect this at your local highway stop any time soon.
    forYou: >-
      If you're buying an EV in India today, judge it on real-world range and the chargers near you, not on megawatt headlines from China.

    catch: >-

    kind: myth-buster
    heading: "Your phone isn't listening to you for ads"
    body: >-
      The idea that your phone secretly records conversations to target ads refuses to die. Researchers who tested more than 17,000 Android apps found no hidden audio recording, and an always-on mic would show the orange or green indicator and drain your battery. What actually targets you is location, searches, contacts and what people around you looked up, which is a duller explanation and a creepier one.
    problem: >-
      You talk about a product, and an ad for it shows up an hour later. It feels like proof that the phone was listening, and that feeling is why the myth keeps spreading.
    breakthrough: >-
      A Northeastern University study of more than 17,000 Android apps found no evidence of secret audio being sent to advertisers, though it did catch some apps sharing screenshots and screen recordings. Both iOS and Android now show a coloured indicator whenever the microphone is live, and streaming audio all day would be very visible in battery use.
    catch: >-
      In 2024 a US marketing firm pitched an 'Active Listening' ad product to clients, and Google cut ties with it after the story broke. There's no evidence it worked at scale, but it's a reason to keep an eye on mic permissions anyway.
    forYou: >-
      Spend five minutes on the settings that matter: turn off ad personalisation in your Google and Meta accounts, and change any app that has location set to Always. Review microphone permissions while you're there.
