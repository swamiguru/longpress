    body: >-
      We're barely half through 2026 and US data-breach notices have already topped 471 million — more than in all of 2025. The uncomfortable part: most of it isn't elite hacking. It's scam calls (vishing) and passwords reused across sites. You can't stop companies from leaking your data, but reused passwords are the one link fully in your control — so a password manager and unique logins are the highest-leverage move you can make today.
    problem: >-
      Everyone assumes breaches are the work of genius hackers cracking unbreakable systems — so it feels hopeless and out of our hands. That framing makes people shrug and do nothing.
    breakthrough: >-
      The Identity Theft Resource Center's H1 2026 report shows US compromises on pace to smash the record: 1,803 incidents and 471M+ victim notices by midyear — already above the 297.5M for all of 2025. The main drivers are credential theft, vishing (voice-phishing scam calls) and reused passwords, not exotic zero-days.
    catch: >-
      'Notices' isn't the same as unique victims — one person can receive several, so the human count is lower than 471M. And no personal habit protects you when a company you trusted is the one that leaks.
    forYou: >-
      Do the two things that actually blunt this: use a password manager with a unique password per site, and never approve an OTP or payment from an inbound call or text. Those alone neutralize most of what's driving 2026's numbers.

    heading: "One Samsung phone, two audio outputs at once"
    body: >-
      Samsung's Separate App Sound has been hiding in the settings since the Galaxy S8, and almost nobody uses it. It sends one app's audio to a different output than everything else — so Maps navigation can play through your car speaker while your music streams to your earbuds at the same time. Find it under Settings > Sounds and vibration > Separate app sound, pick the app and the output device, and you're done.
    problem: >-
      By default a phone sends all audio to one place, so navigation, music and calls constantly fight over the same output — annoying when you want directions out loud but music somewhere private.
    breakthrough: >-
      Samsung's Separate App Sound (Galaxy S8 and newer, Android 7+) lets you route a single chosen app to a different audio device while the rest of the phone keeps using its main output. Set it in Settings > Sounds and vibration > Separate app sound.
    catch: >-
      It only works when the second output is genuinely different from the main one (a connected Bluetooth speaker or earbuds), it's Samsung-only, and just one app can be split at a time.
    forYou: >-
      If you're on a Galaxy, turn it on for your maps or music app before your next drive — nav on the car speakers, everything else in your ears.

    kind: myth-buster
    heading: "More RAM won't fix your phone"
    body: >-
      '16GB RAM?! That'll be so fast' — not really. Once a phone has enough RAM, the extra mostly sits idle. What actually makes a phone feel quick is smart memory management, a strong efficient chip, and fast storage — not a bigger number on the box. 8GB handled well can beat 16GB handled badly, so don't pay flagship money chasing RAM you'll never use.
    problem: >-
      RAM is the easiest spec to market, so brands lean on huge numbers and buyers assume more RAM automatically means a faster phone — and overpay for it.
    breakthrough: >-
      In practice, modern Android and iOS aggressively manage memory, so past a comfortable amount (roughly 8-12GB for most people) extra RAM largely stays parked. Real-world speed comes from the chip, storage type (UFS/NVMe) and how well the software handles memory.
    catch: >-
      RAM isn't useless — heavy multitaskers, gamers and people juggling dozens of apps do benefit from headroom, and some cheap phones cut RAM too far. 'Enough' depends on how you use it; the point is the ceiling is lower than the spec sheet implies.
    forYou: >-
      Buy for real-world performance: prioritize the chip and storage, pick a sensible amount of RAM for how you actually use your phone, and don't spend extra just to see a bigger number.
