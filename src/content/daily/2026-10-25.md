    heading: "Rs 14 crore slipped out through the pipe every bank shares"
    body: >-
      Central Bank of India says 187 unauthorised transfers took Rs 13.97 crore out of its settlement account with the RBI between October 15 and 17, and none of them exist in its branch, net banking or core banking records, as [The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/banking/central-bank-of-india-says-unknown-persons-siphoned-rs-13-97-crore) reported. That missing trail is the real story. Investigators suspect someone got into the SFMS messaging link that carries RTGS and NEFT instructions to IFTAS, the RBI subsidiary that runs it, and the money landed in 36 different banks. The complaint says no retail customer was debited directly, so this one lands on the bank rather than on you.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-10-25/illustration_1.png"
    problem: >-
      Every bank's large interbank payments travel over the same messaging rails to the RBI. Customers never see that layer, and most of India's fraud conversation is about phones and OTPs, not about it.
    breakthrough: >-
      Between October 15 and 17, 187 transfers (6, then 88, then 93) moved Rs 13.97 crore from Central Bank of India's RBI settlement account into accounts at 36 banks, none carrying the bank's usual internal transaction IDs, per [The420](https://the420.in/central-bank-of-india-rbi-settlement-account-cyber-fraud/). Mumbai's Nodal Cyber Police registered an FIR, and the bank alerted the RBI, IFTAS, NCIIPC and CERT-In.
    catch: >-
      Nobody has said how the attackers got in, and police admit they have very little to go on. Until IFTAS or the RBI explains what failed, every other bank on the same rails is left wondering whether the same door is open for it too, which is not a comfortable place for a payments system to be.
    forYou: >-
      If you bank with Central Bank of India, nothing in the complaint points at customer accounts, so ignore any 'your account was affected, verify now' call that follows this headline. The thing worth watching is whether the RBI tells the public what broke.
    question: "How was Rs 13.97 crore stolen from Central Bank of India's RBI account?"
    answer: >-
      Between October 15 and 17, 2026, 187 unauthorised transfers moved Rs 13.97 crore out of Central Bank of India's settlement account with the RBI into accounts at 36 banks. Investigators suspect a breach of the SFMS messaging link run by IFTAS. The bank's complaint says no customer account was directly debited.
    qa:
      - q: "Is my money safe in Central Bank of India after the Rs 13.97 crore fraud?"
        a: "Going by the bank's complaint, yes. The 187 transfers came out of the bank's own settlement balance at the RBI, not from retail accounts, and the complaint says the evidence doesn't show direct debits from individual customers. The loss sits with the bank while investigators work out how the payment instructions were sent."
      - q: "What is SFMS and why does the Central Bank of India breach matter?"
        a: "SFMS, the Structured Financial Messaging System, carries the payment messages banks send through the RBI's RTGS and NEFT systems, and it's run by IFTAS, an RBI subsidiary. If fake instructions got through it, the weak point is plumbing that every Indian bank depends on, which is why CERT-In and NCIIPC were alerted."
      - q: "Who is investigating the Central Bank of India settlement account fraud?"
        a: "Mumbai's Nodal Cyber Police Station registered an FIR against unknown persons for cheating and personation after the bank's treasury general manager complained. The bank also informed the RBI, IFTAS, the Daksh portal, NCIIPC and CERT-In. Police say tracing the money is hard because the initial data on the transfers is so thin."

    body: >-
      The Transport Ministry has ordered CERT-In forensic audits of VAHAN, SARATHI, e-Challan and PUCC after reports that vehicle owners' personal details were on sale for as little as Rs 5 per number plate, according to [The420](https://the420.in/transport-ministry-cert-in-audit-vahan-sarathi-data-leak/). An RTI reply shows 25,314 private companies held active login credentials and ran 1.708 crore searches over 33 months. I'm not sure 'leak' is even the right word. When that many companies hold legitimate keys to the database, someone reselling the answers was always going to happen, and no hacker was needed for it.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-10-25/illustration_2.png"
    problem: >-
      Your vehicle registration links your name, address and phone number to a plate anyone can read off the road. The government shares access to that database with tens of thousands of private companies.
    breakthrough: >-
      A Telegram channel reportedly sold owner details, licence and challan records for fees as low as Rs 5 using only a registration number. On October 24 the ministry ordered CERT-In audits of the four portals, asked NIC to audit the logistics platform, asked app stores including Google to pull apps showing owner details, and put government data requests behind the Transport Secretary's sign-off.
    catch: >-
      The audits look at the portals, but the problem the RTI numbers describe is the access policy itself. Unless the 25,314 corporate logins are cut back hard, the same data will keep leaking out through the front door.
    forYou: >-
      You can't opt out, so assume strangers can know your name and address from your plate. A caller quoting those details hasn't proved anything about who they are.
    question: "Why is CERT-In auditing VAHAN and SARATHI over a vehicle data leak?"
    answer: >-
      On October 24, 2026, the Transport Ministry ordered CERT-In forensic audits of VAHAN, SARATHI, e-Challan and PUCC after reports that vehicle owners' names, addresses and phone numbers were sold on Telegram for as little as Rs 5 per number plate. RTI records show 25,314 private firms held access credentials.
    qa:
      - q: "What vehicle owner data was leaked from VAHAN in India?"
        a: "Reports describe a Telegram channel offering owner names, addresses, phone numbers, driving licence and challan records and registration details for as little as Rs 5, using only a vehicle registration number. Two apps, Vehicle Information App and VehicleSetu, were flagged, though download numbers alone don't prove either did anything unlawful."
      - q: "What has the government done about the VAHAN data leak?"
        a: "The Transport Ministry told CERT-In to audit the four portals, asked NIC to audit access on the logistics platform, and asked app stores including Google to remove apps showing owner details. Government requests for logistics data now need the Transport Secretary's personal sign-off plus identity checks by digital signature or video."
      - q: "Can I stop my vehicle details showing up in these apps?"
        a: "Not really, and that's the uncomfortable part. The data comes from the government's own registration systems, so owners can't opt out. Treat any caller who knows your name, address and plate number as unverified, since those details were cheap to buy, and report fraud attempts on Sanchar Saathi's Chakshu."

    heading: "Back from your trip? Switch UPI International off"
    body: >-
      PhonePe lets you switch UPI International off yourself from your profile photo, then Payment Management, International, deactivate, and your UPI PIN, according to [PhonePe's help page](https://cms.phonepe.com/en/myhelp/money-transfers/upi-international/how-do-i-deactivate-upi-international/). Left alone, it stays on for 90 days after you activate it. That's fine for a long trip and pointless for a long weekend in Bangkok, because it's one more spending channel left open on your account. On a day with two data and fraud stories in the brief, closing the doors you aren't using feels like basic hygiene.
    image: "/images/daily/2026-10-25/illustration_3.png"
    problem: >-
      Most people turn on UPI International for one trip and forget it exists. PhonePe keeps it live for up to 90 days, long after you've unpacked.
    breakthrough: >-
      The deactivate option sits in the same menu you used to turn it on: profile photo, Payment Management, International, deactivate, confirm, UPI PIN. PhonePe's help pages confirm both the steps and the 90-day automatic switch-off, and [turning it back on](https://cms.phonepe.com/en/myhelp/money-transfers/upi-international/how-do-i-activate-upi-international-feature-phonepe/) takes the same four taps.
    catch: >-
      It won't protect you if someone already has your UPI PIN, since domestic payments still work. It just narrows what a stolen PIN can be used for.
    forYou: >-
      Check it today if you travelled this year, and make switching it off part of unpacking. If you activated it in another UPI app instead, look for the same setting there.
    question: "How do I turn off UPI International in PhonePe?"
    answer: >-
      In PhonePe, tap your profile photo, open International under Payment Management, choose deactivate, confirm and enter your UPI PIN. PhonePe also turns UPI International off automatically 90 days after you activate it, so switching it off yourself matters when you're home sooner than that.
    qa:
      - q: "Does UPI International in PhonePe turn off automatically?"
        a: "Yes. PhonePe's help page says UPI International switches off on its own 90 days after the activation date. That means a two-week holiday leaves it live for roughly another ten weeks unless you deactivate it yourself from Payment Management and then International in the app."
      - q: "How do I turn UPI International back on in PhonePe for a trip?"
        a: "Tap your profile photo, go to International under Payment Management, tap Activate next to the bank account you want to use abroad, and enter your UPI PIN. Do it a day before you fly so you're not fiddling with settings at a shop counter, then switch it off again once you're home."

      PhonePe and Google Pay processed 77.8% of listed-app UPI volume in September 2026, down from 81.9% a year earlier, per [MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/10/223-phonepe-google-pay-upi-share-sept-2026/).
